Perspective and incidence

A planar mystery, a spatial proof.

Two triangles lie in distinct planes. Their corresponding vertices line up with O. Where can the intersections of corresponding sides lie?

Teal: ABC. Rose: A′B′C′. Gold circles: corresponding side intersections. In the spatial view, drag to orbit. In the planar view, drag A, B or C: the triangle A′B′C′ follows, and P, Q, R stay on the gold axis. The planes are drawn as wireframes so the full incidence configuration remains visible.

Why are the three intersections collinear?

O, A, and B span a plane containing both AB and A′B′, so those side lines meet projectively. Their intersection belongs to both triangle planes. The same reasoning applies to AC and BC. All three intersections therefore lie on the line where the two triangle planes meet.

Central projection preserves these incidences. The planar view is the actual projection of this spatial configuration, not an independently drawn approximation. An arbitrary synthetic projective plane need not embed in three-dimensional projective space: this is why Desargues can fail in a general projective plane.

The centre O and the two planes are fixed; A, B, C move in the first plane and A′, B′, C′ are where the lines from O meet the second. A side intersection leaves the picture when two corresponding sides become nearly parallel: it is then near infinity, still on the axis.
