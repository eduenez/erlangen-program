Groups Generated by Translations

The group itself, and its action on the plane — two different pictures

A geometric transformation group is a set of transformations of a space that forms a group under composition — closed, with an identity and inverses. This lesson deliberately keeps two pictures separate, because conflating them is the single easiest place to get confused about groups of transformations:

  • The group itself is abstract. Every translation happens to correspond to exactly one vector, so once you fix an origin, the group can be drawn as a collection of arrows out of that point — one arrow per group element. This picture needs a distinguished origin; it is a picture of vectors, not of geometry.
  • The group's action on the plane is what you get by applying every one of those transformations to some object. The result — an orbit — needs no origin at all: translating a shape that starts anywhere still produces a perfectly good lattice of copies.

Below, the same generators drive both pictures at once, so you can watch the first (vectors from a fixed origin) and the second (an orbit you can drag anywhere) stay in lock-step as you change the generators.

Try it:
  • In Picture 1, only two arrows are bold (blue = v1, red = v2) — every other arrow is a light gray combination of them. Increase N and watch how quickly the bold pair gets buried among their combinations. The group only needs two generators; showing all of it needs infinitely many arrows, of which this is a finite crop.
  • In Picture 2, drag the shape far from the origin, then switch between Square and Hexagonal. The orbit's shape (square-packed vs. hexagonally-packed) doesn't care at all where you dropped the object — only the generators decide that.
  • Switch to Custom and drag v1, v2 close to parallel in Picture 1's sliders. Watch Picture 1's arrows collapse toward a line and Picture 2's orbit collapse the same way, simultaneously — two views of exactly one thing degenerating.

The same lattice, different generators

A subtlety neither picture shows directly: different pairs (v1, v2) can generate the same lattice — the same set of points, just reached by different combinations. For instance, (v1, v2) and (v1 + v2, v2) always generate the same lattice, because m·v1 + n·v2 = m·(v1+v2) + (n−m)·v2 — every point reachable with one pair is reachable with the other. Picture 1 would look different for the two pairs (different arrows drawn), while Picture 2's orbit — the actual set of points reached — would be identical.

Summary
  • A group of translations is, once you fix an origin, a set of vectors — that's Picture 1, and it is the picture that needs the origin.
  • The same group's action on a point or shape is an orbit, and an orbit has no privileged basepoint — Picture 2 works identically no matter where you drag the shape, which is exactly what makes it the right picture for "the group doing something to the plane" rather than "the group by itself."
  • Two independent generators give a lattice; the abstract group is always ℤ × ℤ, but the geometric picture (square, hexagonal, or slanted) depends entirely on the specific vectors chosen.
  • Different generator pairs can produce the identical lattice — "the group" and "a set of generators for the group" are not the same thing.
Exercises
  1. Why Picture 1 needs an origin and Picture 2 doesn't. Explain, in one or two sentences, why moving the "origin" in Picture 1 would change every arrow drawn, while moving Picture 2's shape changes nothing about the set of transformations being depicted — only which particular orbit you're looking at.
  2. Degenerate case. Set Custom v1, v2 to the exact same angle (parallel vectors). What does the "lattice" actually look like in both pictures, and why does it fail to be 2-dimensional?
  3. Same lattice, found two ways. Pick your own v1, v2, then predict what (2v1 + v2, v1 + v2) generates before checking. Same lattice, a sublattice, or something else?
  4. Density. For the square lattice, how many lattice points lie within distance R of the origin, as a function of R?
